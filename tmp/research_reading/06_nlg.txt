--- PAGE 18 ---
Side note – exposure bias
Training with teacher forcing leads to exposure bias at generation time
● During training, our model’s inputs are gold context tokens from real, 
human-generated texts
● At generation time, our inputs are previously–decoded tokens


--- PAGE 19 ---
Exposure Bias Solutions
● Scheduled sampling (Bengio et al., 2015)
○ With some probability p, decode a token and feed that as the text input 
rather than the gold token.
○ Increase p over the course of training
○ Leads to improvements but can create strange training objectives.
● Reinforcement learning: cast your text generation model as a Markov 
decision process
○ Rewards are provided by an external source (e.g., BLEU, ROUGE)
○ Learn behaviors by rewarding the model when it exhibits them
○ Be careful about optimizing for the task vs. “gaming” the reward!
■ Evaluation metrics are merely proxies for generation quality!

Beam Search
● We want to find a sequence with maximum likelihood
○ But exhaustive search is infeasible (exponential with T)
● Core idea: On each step of decoder, keep track of the k most probable 
partial sequences (which we call hypotheses)
○ Score them by their log-probability
○ Scores are all negative, and higher score is better
● Beam search is not guaranteed to find optimal solution
○ But much more efficient than exhaustive search!

Beam search: stopping criteria
● In greedy decoding, usually we decode until the model produces an 
<END> token. For example: <START>he hit me with a pie<END>
● In beam search decoding, different hypotheses may produce <END> 
tokens on different timesteps
○ When a hypothesis produces <END>, that hypothesis is complete.
○ Place it aside & continue exploring other hypotheses via beam search.
● Usually we continue beam search until:
○ We reach timestep T (where T is some a predefined cutoff),or
○ We have at least n completed hypotheses (n is a predefined cutoff)
● Problem: longer hypotheses have lower scores. Why?
○ Fix: Normalize by length

Top-k sampling
Problem: Vanilla sampling makes every token in the vocabulary an option
● If the probability to get ”bad” tokens is 1%, after 500 tokens, the probability we 
only sampled good tokens is less than 1% 
● Once a “bad” token is chosen, things spiral fast..
● Even if most of the probability mass in the distribution is over a limited set of 
options, the tail of the distribution could be very long and in aggregate have 
considerable mass (statistics speak: we have “heavy tailed” distributions)
● Many tokens are probably really wrong in the current context
Solution: Only sample from the top-k tokens

--- PAGE 53 ---
Top-p (nucleus) sampling
Problem: Probability distributions are dynamic
● When P is flatter, a limited k removes many viable options
● When P it peakier, a high k allows for too many options to have a chance of 
being selected
Solution: Top-p sampling
● Sample from all tokens in the top-p cumulative probability mass (i.e., where 
mass is concentrated)
● Varies k depending on the uniformity of P

--- PAGE 57 ---
Scaling randomness: Temperature
● On timestamp t, the model computes a probability distribution Pt by 
applying the softmax function to a vector of scores
● Re-distribute the probability with the temperature coefficient 𝜏
● Large 𝜏 values result in more diverse outputs
○ For any 𝜏 < 1 we start getting less diverse outputs
○ When 𝜏 → ∞ we get a uniform distribution over the vocabulary
○ When 𝜏 → 0 we get the argmax distribution

Improving decoding with re-ranking
● Problem: What if I decode a bad sequence from my model?
● Solution: Decode a bunch of sequences!
○ 10 candidates is a common number, but it’s up to you
● Define a score to approximate quality of sequences and
○ Simplest is to use (low) perplexity!
○ Careful! repetitive utterances generally get low perplexity
● Re-rankers can score a variety of properties:
○ Style (Holtzman et al., 2018) ,discourse(Gabriel et al., 2021), 
entailment/factuality(Goyal et al., 2020), logical consistency (Lu et al., 2020)
○ Beware of poorly-calibrated re-rankers
● Can compose multiple re-rankers together.

--- PAGE 72 ---
Content overlap metrics
● Compute a score that indicates the lexical similarity between generated 
and gold (human-written) text
● Fast, efficient, automatic and widely used
● N-gram overlap metrics (e.g., BLEU, ROUGE, METEOR, CIDEr, etc.)
● Fine for short-text translation with multiple references.
○ Unaligned for long-text generations such as summarization, even 
worse for dialog or story generation
○ But still widely used ☹
 


--- PAGE 73 ---
ROUGE
● Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation (Lin 2004) 
○ A set of metrics and a software package specifically designed for 
evaluating automatic summarization
● ROUGE-N measures the number of matching n-grams between the 
model-generated text and a human-produced reference.
○ Computes both precision and recall to get an F1 score.

--- PAGE 74 ---
ROUGE
Reference: The cat is on the mat ↔ Candidate: The cat and the dog
 
  
  
Rouge-L: Longest common subsequence, i.e. the longest sequence of words 
(not necessarily consecutive, but still in order) that is shared between both.
- ROUGE-L precision is the ratio of the length of the LCS, over the unigrams in C
- ROUGE-L recall is the ratio of the length of the LCS, over the unigrams in R.

Evaluation: Takeaways
● Content overlap metrics provide a good starting point for evaluating the 
quality of generated text, but they’re not good enough on their own.
● Model-based metrics are often more correlated with human judgment, 
but behavior is not interpretable.
● Human judgments are critical, but humans are inconsistent!
● Using LLMs as judges seems like a promising direction.
● In many cases, the best judge of output quality is YOU!
○ Look at your model generations. Don’t just rely on numbers!
○ Publish many samples of the output of systems that you create!
